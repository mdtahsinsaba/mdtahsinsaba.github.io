India's economy has undergone a remarkable transformation, evolving from a relatively closed and heavily regulated economy into a more globally integrated and market-oriented economic system. However, this transformation has not translated into equivalent improvements in living standards for all sections of society.

The roots of economic inequality can partly be traced to the colonial period, when land ownership and productive resources were concentrated in the hands of a relatively small section of the population. Following independence, the Indian state adopted redistributive measures, including land reforms and state-led economic policies, to address these structural inequalities. Nevertheless, institutional constraints, uneven implementation of land reforms and the restrictive economic framework associated with the Licence Raj limited the extent of structural transformation.

Although economic liberalisation accelerated growth and expanded market opportunities, its benefits have not been distributed equally. Unequal ownership of productive assets, disparities in employment opportunities and differences in earning capacity have contributed to the concentration of wealth and income.
Consequently, a substantial section of the population continues to face economic insecurity. The extensive coverage of the Public Distribution System reflects the importance of state intervention in ensuring access to essential food grains. While subsidised food distribution provides an important social safety net, persistent nutritional and affordability challenges indicate that economic growth alone is insufficient to guarantee food security.

##Therefore, India's development challenge is not merely to sustain economic growth, but to ensure that its benefits translate into broad-based improvements in human welfare.##
